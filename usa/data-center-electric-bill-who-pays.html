The data center bill 417 House members voted for, and 43 senators killed

It asked states to consider protecting people from a cost it never assigned to anyone. Meanwhile 23 states wrote their own tariffs — starting at a fifth of the load the federal bill covers.

By Daniel Reyes · 14 November 2026 · 9 min read · Standards

The short version

  • The House passed the Ratepayer Protection Act 417 to 3 on 16 September. The entire Democratic House leadership voted yes.
  • The Senate killed it 57 to 43 on 30 September. Only four Democrats defected. Senator Chuck Schumer called it "toothless."
  • It was toothless, measurably: it only asked states to consider protections, and only above 100 megawatts.
  • Twenty-three states already wrote tariffs that start at 20 to 25 megawatts and charge the developer.

On 13 November, Pennsylvania Democrats were still counting a state Senate majority they had not held in more than thirty years. Opposition to data centres was central to the campaigns that delivered it. "I've never in my career seen anything as unpopular as data centers," Molly Parzen of Conservation Voters of Pennsylvania told Inside Climate News.

Five weeks earlier, both chambers of Congress had already voted on the same grievance. One chamber voted almost unanimously to fix it. The other refused.

The vote

HR 9340, the Ratepayer Protection Act, was introduced by Senator Jon Husted, an Ohio Republican, in July. It passed the House on 16 September by 417 votes to 3. Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez voted yes. So did Ilhan Omar, Hakeem Jeffries, Nancy Pelosi and every member of the Democratic House leadership.

Husted took it to the Senate floor the next day and asked for unanimous consent. A Democrat objected. On 30 September he tried again on a cloture motion. It failed 57 to 43 — three votes short of sixty. Forty-three Democrats voted no; four crossed.

A bill that got 99.3 percent of the House died on a three-vote margin in the Senate.

Husted's floor speech is worth reading for its frustration: "All we have to do is take yes for an answer." Schumer's reply, per Roll Call, was that the bill was "toothless."

What the bill actually covered

Schumer was not being rhetorical. Read the text against the state tariffs already on the books and the word is a description.

Row one is the whole argument. A 99-megawatt facility — a large one — sits comfortably inside Virginia's tariff and outside the federal bill entirely. Row three is the other half: the bill directs no one to pay for anything.

The instrument it would have applied is real, and it works. Virginia's GS-5 tariff, effective January 2027, requires 14-year minimum contracts, 85 percent of contracted transmission demand, 60 percent of contracted generation demand, and $1.5 million per megawatt in collateral — $75 million on a 50-megawatt project. Oregon's Schedule 96 makes data centres over 20 megawatts cover the full cost of distribution upgrades.

Twenty-three states didn't wait

As of May 2026, the Edison Electric Institute counted 23 states that had approved at least one large-load tariff aimed specifically at data centre customers, with seven more pending. Ohio's commission approved one for AEP in July. Kansas approved one. Pennsylvania's PPL settlement carried one; the state PUC adopted a framework in April. Oklahoma signed legislation in June that explicitly protects residential and commercial ratepayers.

This is the awkward part for both sides. The Republican bill was redundant before it reached the floor. The Democratic objection — make it mandatory — describes what 23 states have already done voluntarily and what Virginia made binding with a collateral clause.

The money that moves, and the money that doesn't

Tariffs are about cost that hasn't arrived. There is a second channel charging households right now, and almost nobody is looking at it.

PJM Interconnection runs the largest grid in the country, serving more than 65 million people. Its capacity auction price rose roughly tenfold between mid-2024 and mid-2025. PJM's own independent market monitor, Monitoring Analytics, called data centres "the primary reason."

Ratepayers are set to spend an extra $7.3 billion in capacity charges this delivery year and $6.5 billion the next, according to the monitor's January report. Capacity is only 10 to 20 percent of a bill, but the increase still reached $16 to $18 a month in Ohio and western Maryland, of which 63 percent is attributed to data centres.

Call it ten or eleven dollars a month. That money left household accounts while the Senate was debating a bill that would have changed nothing this year.

The pledge is not a mandate, and the President said it was

In March the White House published a Ratepayer Protection Pledge. Amazon, Google, Meta, Microsoft, OpenAI, Oracle and xAI signed it. On 21 October, President Trump posted that data centres "are mandated by me to make their own electricity."

They aren't. The pledge is voluntary and unenforced.

"It's nothing. It's just a messaging document."

Paul Donohoo-Vallett, former Department of Energy official

"It was a PR stunt."

John Quigley, Kleinman Center for Energy Policy, University of Pennsylvania

"Compliance is not being monitored or enforced by anyone."

Ari Peskoe, Electricity Law Initiative, Harvard

Who loses

A 100-megawatt threshold protects hyperscalers and abandons everyone smaller. A 60-megawatt colocation operator in a state without a tariff gets no federal cover, no state cover, and queues for interconnection against the same constrained grid. The bill was written for the seven companies that signed the pledge, which is a reasonable explanation for why they signed it.

The counterargument, at its strongest

The industry and the Department of Energy have a real case, and it deserves stating before it is knocked down.

The Electric Power Research Institute, which is funded largely by utilities and is therefore not a friendly witness, estimated in a June study that data centres lowered average retail rates by 6 percent between 2015 and 2024 for customers in average-growth areas. Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory and the Brattle Group found retail prices rose 29 percent from 2019 to 2025 but only 3 percent once adjusted for inflation, and that load growth was mostly associated with lower prices through 2025.

On the national average, they are broadly right. The rebuttal is distributional, not directional.

"To date, except for in the PJM market, load growth and data centers in particular... have not been a principal driver of price increases," Douglas Arent of Columbia's Center on Global Energy Policy told FactCheck.org — and then named the exception. Sanya Carley at Penn found the PJM increases added at least $10 a month in western Maryland and Ohio from mid-2025.

The averages are also lagging the build. "Studies that examine effects of data centers on prices in the past are largely irrelevant," Peskoe said. "Today's data centers use 10 to 100x more electricity than the previous generation."

What would actually fix it

Not a pledge, and not a bill that says "consider." The elements that show up in the tariffs with teeth are specific: a load threshold low enough to catch mid-size projects, full assignment of upgrade cost to the developer, long minimum terms, collateral sized to the interconnection, exit fees, and curtailment rights so a strained grid can shed the load first.

Yale energy economist Kenneth Gillingham, reviewing the House bill, put the limit plainly: the ideas are "very reasonable," but "implementation will really determine how much and/or if it will help."

Questions

Did the Senate actually kill the Ratepayer Protection Act?

Yes, for this Congress. A cloture motion failed 57 to 43 on 30 September 2026, three votes short of the sixty needed. Forty-three Democrats voted no. An earlier unanimous-consent request had already been objected to on 17 September.

How big does a data centre have to be before the federal bill applies?

100 megawatts. State tariffs in force typically start at 20 to 25 megawatts, so the federal threshold is four to five times higher than the state rules it was meant to nudge.

Is there a stricter bill?

Yes. The GRID Act, filed by Senator Josh Hawley of Missouri with Senator Richard Blumenthal of Connecticut in February 2026, goes further. It has not left committee.

Do data centres raise electricity bills or not?

Both, depending where you live. Nationally the average effect is small and possibly negative. In the PJM region the effect is documented and already being billed — roughly $10 to $11 a month attributable in Ohio and western Maryland.

What happens next

Pennsylvania's new Senate majority is seated in January and has said it will move on moratoriums and on repealing the state's data centre sales-tax exemption, which the state estimates could cost about $2 billion in revenue by mid-2031. Watch the January session calendar, not the federal one. On the federal side the only live trigger is whether Husted refiles in the new Congress — and whether the four Democrats who crossed become five.
