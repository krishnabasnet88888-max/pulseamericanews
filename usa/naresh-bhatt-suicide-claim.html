Naresh Bhatt's suicide defence: what he claimed, and why the jury refused it

He conceded the saw and the bin. He disputed only the cause of death. The theory hung together on paper — and it took the jury about four hours to decide it did not hang together in fact.

By Daniel Reyes· 16 November 2026·6 min read

The claim: Bhatt testified that his wife died by suicide at home, that he found her, and that he dismembered her body with a power saw in a panic and put the remains out with the household rubbish. Asked whether he had defiled and concealed her body, he said yes to both. Asked whether he killed her, he said: "No, not even imagination."

Bhatt testified in his own defence that his wife died by suicide at home and that he dismembered her afterwards. He did not call an ambulance, a doctor or her family before starting. Under that account the disposal was a reaction to a death, not a concealment of a killing.

Why the theory is not stupid

It is worth being clear about what the defence did here, because it was deliberate rather than desperate. Bhatt conceded the two facts that were physically undeniable — the saw and the bin — and contested only the one fact that could not be proved without a body: the cause of death.

That is a rational structure. If the death was a suicide, then concealing a body and defiling a body are explained as tidying up after a tragedy. The murder count collapses. The two Class 6 counts follow the concession and are worth at most ten years between them.

The concession also had a defensive purpose. By admitting the dismemberment he removed the jury's obvious question — why would a man cut up his wife? — and spent it on his own terms.

Where it broke down

Four problems, none of them forensic in isolation.

He did not call anyone

The defence account requires a man to find his wife dead and reach for a saw before reaching for a phone. No ambulance, no doctor, no call to her family in Nepal, no call to her colleagues at the hospital where she had failed to appear. Each of those is a person who would have asked questions.

The house did not look like a suicide scene

Prosecutors put blood and cleanup evidence before the jury. A suicide in a home leaves a scene; a killing followed by a cleanup leaves a different one. The state's case was that the second is what jurors were looking at.

Her digital life stopped dead

Phone and message traffic ended abruptly rather than tapering. A woman who had been job-hunting, messaging and planning does not, on the defence account, stop mid-conversation — she stops because the phone stopped with her.

Credibility, not forensics

This is the one that decided it. A juror, asked afterwards what produced the verdict, did not talk about blood or compaction. The line was about honesty.

So many lies were told.

Juror, speaking to WUSA9 after the verdict

Four hours is fast for a case with seventeen days of testimony and a live defendant. Fast verdicts on a contested cause of death read as a jury that resolved the witness, not the science.

The search history

One detail sits awkwardly for both sides. In the small hours of 30 July 2024, Bhatt's phone searched "diagram of brain" and "chicken farm near me."

Note that the second one helps neither side particularly: on the defence account he needed somewhere to put a body he had not caused; on the state's account he needed somewhere to put a body he had. It is evidence of planning either way, which is why it landed hard.

What it means for appeal

The suicide theory survives the verdict as a ground of appeal on the two lesser counts. The murder count is armoured by his own concession — he cannot now argue the dismemberment did not happen — so any challenge must attack sufficiency rather than fact.

That argument runs through the sentencing arithmetic, where the difference between concurrent and consecutive terms is the difference between a release date and none. Common questions

What was Bhatt's defence?

That his wife died by suicide and that he dismembered her afterwards in a panic.

Did he admit dismembering her?

Yes. He conceded the power saw and the disposal on the stand, and answered yes when asked if he defiled and concealed her body.

Why did the jury reject it?

A juror framed the verdict around credibility — "so many lies were told" — rather than forensics. The jury took about four hours.
