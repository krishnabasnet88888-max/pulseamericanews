Maine's electric bill rose 10.8% — the third-largest mainland increase, with no tariff to show for it

Maine's residential rate reached 22.28 cents a kilowatt-hour in August 2026, second only to Massachusetts in New England and third-highest rise in the mainland. Maine has no large-load data center instrument. It also has almost no data centers.

Maine is the counter-example that matters. If data centers were the whole story, Maine should be flat: it hosts a trivial share of national data center capacity and has no large-load tariff because it has had no large load to tariff. Instead it rose 10.8%, faster than Ohio, which hosts far more.

The cause is structural. Maine generates little of its own power, imports most of it across constrained ties into ISO-NE, and pays a renewable portfolio standard on the bill. When ISO-NE capacity clears higher — for the same data center reasons as PJM — Maine pays more for the power it imports and gets no offsetting economic benefit from the load.

The distributional point

This is the sharpest version of the argument in the whole country. A Maine household is paying for grid investment driven by load in Massachusetts, Connecticut and the Boston corridor, on a grid it does not control, for campuses it will never see. There is no host-community upside: no property tax, no jobs, no municipal fibre.

That is not an argument against data centers. It is an argument for state instruments that assign upgrade cost to the developer rather than the rate base, because the rate base in Maine is mostly other people.

What Maine could copy

Oregon's Schedule 96, at 20 megawatts, requires a facility to fund the full cost of the distribution upgrades it triggers. Virginia's GS-5 goes further: $1.5 million a megawatt in collateral, a 14-year term, 85% of the transmission demand covered. The terms are here.

Both are cheap to copy and neither requires a data center to exist first. A tariff written before the load arrives is a condition of entry rather than a remedy.

What to do if your bill jumped

  • Get the line items. A rise in the supply line is a market event; a rise in riders is the standard-setting decision; a rise in the fixed charge is a rate case.
  • Compare to last August. Maine's seasonal swing is large and month-to-month comparisons mislead.
  • Comment at the PUC. Maine has no large-load docket yet. That is the reason to write.
  • Ask about the standard offer. If you are on it, the reset date is the date that matters, not the monthly bill.

Questions

Why did my Maine electric bill go up?

ISO-NE capacity charge plus constrained import capacity. Maine generates little in-state and imports most of its power, and its renewable portfolio standard is paid on the bill.

Does Maine have a data center tariff?

No. Maine is one of 28 states with no large-load instrument, and rose 10.8% — third-largest in the mainland.

How much did Maine electricity rise?

10.8% in the year to August 2026, from 20.11 to 22.28 cents a kilowatt-hour.

Is Maine electricity expensive?

Yes. 22.28 cents against a national average of 18.16, ranking 46th cheapest of 51. Only California, Connecticut, Rhode Island, Massachusetts and Hawaii are higher.
