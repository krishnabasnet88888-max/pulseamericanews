What Naresh Bhatt's conviction actually carries — and why a missing body is still a legal problem

Twenty years to life is a floor, not an answer. The decision that sets his release date is whether two Class 6 felonies run concurrently or consecutively. Under it sits a doctrine about building a murder case with no corpse, and four unresolved problems a verdict does not close.

By Daniel Reyes · 16 November 2026 · 9 min read

The short version

  • Count one carries 20 years to life. Fixed floor, no fine option, no discretion downward.
  • Counts two and three carry up to five years each — and decide whether he has a release date at all.
  • Virginia abolished parole in 1995. Life means life, apart from discretionary geriatric release at 60 or 65.
  • The case was built without a corpse under the corpus delicti corroboration rule.
  • Four things stay unresolved: the defiling count's proof, the death certificate, the estate, the appeal.

The three counts, and the job each does

First-degree murder is a Class 2 felony under Virginia Code § 18.2-32 — wilful, deliberate and premeditated killing, or killing in the commission of certain enumerated felonies. Punishment is fixed at 20 years to life. A judge has no power to go under twenty.

Concealing a dead body (§ 18.2-323.1) and physically defiling a dead body (§ 18.2-323.2) are Class 6 felonies: one to five years each, or up to twelve months and a $2,500 fine at the court's option.

They read as trivial beside a life term. They are not, and the reason is structural.

The arithmetic

Virginia abolished discretionary parole in 1995. For offences after that date a life sentence is served in full, and release runs only through geriatric release under § 53.1-40.01 — generally at 65 after five years served, or 60 after ten, discretionary in both cases.

Sentencing in Virginia is bifurcated: the jury that convicted hears evidence and recommends; the judge may reduce the recommendation but not increase it. The recommendation is advisory. Three configurations are realistically available on 12 March:

  • Concurrent. The Class 6 terms are absorbed into the murder term. Exposure is 20 to life and geriatric release stays theoretically open.
  • Consecutive. The floor moves from 20 years to 22, and the extra decade is bolted onto a tail that may never be reached.
  • High term plus consecutive defiling. The outcome that turns "20 to life" into "life, with a cushion" and makes release academic.

The legal question underneath is narrow and genuinely arguable: is dismemberment aggravating in its own right, or merely the factual basis for charges already counted? Virginia's guidelines score prior record, offence gravity and victim factors. There is no dial labelled "dignity of the remains." A prosecutor seeking the extra years must argue the saw and the bin are separate harms, not housekeeping.

Restitution is the quieter variable. Courts can order it, and in a no-body case the categories are oddly specific — landfill operations, dive searches, private search costs, funeral expenses for a funeral that has not happened. Not punishment, but a large figure in a case where a family was billed for looking for its own daughter is visible from the bench.

The doctrine that let this be tried at all

The rule is corpus delicti — "the body of the crime." Not the corpse: the proposition that a crime occurred. It exists so convictions cannot rest on a confession alone, which is why a defendant cannot walk into a station, say "I did it," and be sentenced on that.

Virginia requires corroboration — independent evidence tending to establish the offence. A low bar in practice, and cleared here repeatedly. But it explains the investigation's shape. With no body and no admission to the killing, four categories carried the case: blood and cleanup evidence in the home; the physical signature a dismemberment leaves in a room; the abrupt stop of her phone and message traffic; and the documented waste route.

The landfill operation deserves a note of its own. Refuse in the region goes to a regional landfill and is compacted into discrete daily cells. Establish which collection round took which bin and which cell it was tipped into, and in principle you can dig for a specific load. Investigators attempted exactly that. It failed, because a bagged body going out with household waste is a small object in an enormous volume and the compaction is brutal.

Understand what the dig was for. Every other category proves that she died and roughly when. Only the body proves how. Recovered remains would have settled cause of death and with it the distance between murder and the suicide account Bhatt gave.

What a verdict does not fix

1. The defiling count is proved by inference. The offence requires a body to have been defiled. Proof rests on his own testimony and trace evidence — enough to convict, but the count most exposed on appeal precisely because its object was never produced.

2. Sufficiency. Any appeal attacks the weakest link. The murder count is armoured by the concession; the Class 6 counts are not.

3. The death certificate. Virginia can issue one on a judicial finding, but without remains the process is slower and easier to contest — and the certificate is what unlocks the estate.

4. The estate. A convicted killer not yet procedurally disentangled from his victim's affairs can sit inside them: the house, the accounts, the insurance. Slayer statutes exist for this; applying them takes time and a separate proceeding, and time is what the family has already spent two years losing.

Add the child, whose custody, maternal estate and survivor benefit no verdict settles, and any future civil action, which needs the same predicate facts and is easier with a grave than without.

None of this says the conviction is fragile. It says guilty and resolved are different words.

What to watch

12 March and the impact statements. Sentencing is the only point where the absence of a body can be described rather than proved. Watch whether the judge treats the disposal as a separate harm.

The consecutive question. The whole sentence lives here — a decade, and whether there is a release date at all.

The certificate and the estate. Quiet filings, no cameras, more informative than the sentencing about the family's next two years.
