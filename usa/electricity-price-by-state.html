Electricity prices in all 50 states in 2027 — and the 23 states charging data centers for it

Maryland is up 11.6% in a year. Nevada is down 2.1% and cheapest in the country at 12.72 cents a kilowatt-hour; Hawaii is dearest at 47.18. The national average is 18.16, and 50 of 51 jurisdictions went up. Here is the full table, then the reason it moved.

The price of a kilowatt-hour is the most localised number in the American economy. It is set by which power plants sit within a few hundred miles of your meter, how much transmission stands between them and you, and what your state legislature has decided to charge for. Nothing else about you matters to it — not your income, not your house, not your credit.

That is why the table below runs from 12.72 cents in Nevada to 47.18 in Hawaii. It is also why the movement in it matters more than the ranking. Fifty of fifty-one jurisdictions rose over the year, and the largest rises were mostly not in the states that were already expensive. They were in Maryland, Maine, Rhode Island, Pennsylvania and Ohio — the corridor carrying the heaviest concentration of new data center load on the eastern grid. The one exception runs the other way: Hawaii's 12.3% is imported fuel and an islanded grid, not load.

Twenty-three states have now passed a large-load instrument in response. Six publish a threshold. The rest are on the books without one, which is a gap in the record rather than a gap in the fact. The full tracker, with every threshold and docket number, is here — the table below marks the six that publish one.

Three limits to state plainly. First, these are state averages — a rural cooperative and a downtown municipal utility in the same state can differ by a factor of two, and the average is neither of them. Second, the price is not the bill: a Hawaii household at 47 cents and a Louisiana household at 13 can pay similar totals if the Louisiana house is larger and air-conditioned through August. Third, a one-year change blends fuel, capacity, weather and policy, and no single year can separate them.

Why the map looks like that

A kilowatt-hour's price is four things stacked: generation, transmission, distribution and policy. Hawaii's 47 cents is almost entirely the first — islanded grids, no interconnection to anything, oil delivered by ship. Nevada's 12.72 is hydro, solar and a low-cost balancing authority. Massachusetts and Rhode Island are the opposite pole: little in-state generation, constrained import capacity, and a renewable portfolio standard paid on the bill.

That explains the shape of the table. It does not explain the movement, which is the part that changed this year and the part a household actually feels.

What pushed 50 states up

The five largest increases were Hawaii +12.3%, Massachusetts +11.9%, Maryland +11.6%, Maine +10.8% and Rhode Island +10.4%. Set Hawaii's islanded grid aside and the four largest mainland increases are all inside PJM or ISO-NE — the two grids carrying the heaviest new load on the eastern seaboard.

PJM Interconnection runs the largest grid in the country, serving more than 65 million people. Its capacity auction price rose roughly tenfold between mid-2024 and mid-2025. Its own independent market monitor, Monitoring Analytics, named data centers the primary reason. The pass-through reached households at $16 to $18 a month in Ohio and western Maryland, of which about 63% is attributed to data centers — call it ten or eleven dollars.

That money left household accounts while the Senate was debating a bill that would have changed nothing this year. The House passed the Ratepayer Protection Act 417–3 on 16 September; the Senate killed it 57–43 on 30 September, three votes short of sixty. The bill's threshold was 100 megawatts, four to five times higher than the state rules it was written to nudge.

Maryland's 11.6% is the second-highest in the country, behind only Hawaii's fuel-driven rise, and it is not a coincidence that Maryland shares a capacity zone with the Loudoun County server corridor. Neither is Pennsylvania's 10.2% — the state's data center build is the reason its Senate majority flipped on 3 November.

The states that got ahead of it

Virginia's GS-5 tariff, effective January, is the template. Any data center above 25 megawatts posts $1.5 million per megawatt in collateral, signs for 14 years, covers 85% of the transmission demand it creates, and pays an exit fee. A 100 MW campus posts $150 million before it breaks ground.

Virginia rose 7.9% — high, but below Maryland, Maine, Rhode Island and Pennsylvania, while absorbing a far larger volume of load than any of them. Oregon, at a 20-megawatt threshold with full distribution-cost assignment, rose 6.3%, below the national average.

Now the honest part. The six states with a published threshold — Oklahoma, Kansas, Oregon, Virginia, Ohio and Pennsylvania — averaged +7.0%. Five PJM states without one — Delaware, Michigan, Illinois, New Jersey and Maryland — averaged +7.6%. The gap is six-tenths of a point and sits inside the noise. One year of data cannot separate tariff design from fuel cost, capacity clearing price and weather.

The claim the tariffs can support is narrower than the claim being made for them: no state with a threshold saw rates fall. The mechanism is a brake, not a reverse gear.

What your bill is actually made of

Most of the arguments about electricity prices are arguments about the wrong line. A residential bill is roughly five components, and only one of them is the one people point at.

  • Generation — the power itself. In competitive markets this is a supply charge; in regulated states it is embedded in the rate base. Typically 50–60% of a bill and the most volatile.
  • Transmission — moving bulk power between regions. In PJM, the capacity and transmission components are where the data center money arrives.
  • Distribution — the poles and wires on your street. Fixed, and rising with replacement cost.
  • Riders and surcharges — state-mandated programmes, storm recovery, energy efficiency. Itemised on some bills, bundled on others.
  • Fixed customer charge — $8 to $25 a month in most states, payable at zero consumption. Rising, because utilities are shifting fixed cost off volumetric rates.

Capacity is only 10 to 20% of a bill. That is exactly why the data center fight is under-covered: the line that moved most is a small slice of a large number, so the total looks like inflation and gets blamed on groceries.

The same fight, in sixteen other countries

Electricity is the one bill where the US sits near the middle of the world. Irish households pay 44.7 cents a kilowatt-hour, 2.5× the US average. Indian households pay 6.8, under two-fifths of it. The top-to-bottom spread on this list is a factor of 6.6, and in every country on it the argument is identical: who pays for the grid a data center needs.

Tier 3 is the real story

A Nigerian household pays 11 cents a kilowatt-hour — 61% of the US average — on a fraction of the income. Kenyan households pay 20, above Brazil and nearly level with the American average, in an economy a fraction of its size.

When a hyperscaler signs a 20-year power purchase agreement in Lagos or Jakarta, it bids for electricity against a grid that already fails. The mechanism is identical to Ohio. The cushion is not. In Ohio a $10 increase is annoying; in Karachi it is a day's wage, and there is no state water bank, no rider schedule and no commission docket to appeal to.

The ratio is the number worth carrying away. Ireland costs 6.6 times India. Both are now being built for the same customer.

The counterargument, at its strongest

The industry and the Department of Energy have a real case and it deserves stating before it is knocked down.

The Electric Power Research Institute — funded largely by utilities, and therefore not a friendly witness — estimated in June that data centers lowered average retail rates by 6% between 2015 and 2024 in average-growth areas. Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory and the Brattle Group found retail prices rose 29% from 2019 to 2025 but only 3% inflation-adjusted, and that load growth was mostly associated with lower prices through 2025.

On the national average they are broadly right. The rebuttal is distributional, not directional. The average is not what lands on a bill in Akron.

Studies that examine effects of data centers on prices in the past are largely irrelevant. Today's data centers use 10 to 100 times more electricity than the previous generation.

Ari Peskoe, Electricity Law Initiative, Harvard

What would actually lower the number

Not a pledge, and not a bill that says "consider." Five elements show up in every tariff with teeth: a load threshold low enough to catch mid-size projects, full assignment of upgrade cost to the developer, long minimum terms, collateral sized to the interconnection, and curtailment rights so a strained grid can shed the load first.

The White House's Ratepayer Protection Pledge has seven signatories and no enforcement. "It's nothing," a former Department of Energy official told us. "It's just a messaging document." Ari Peskoe was blunter: "Compliance is not being monitored or enforced by anyone."

If your bill just jumped: five steps

  1. Get the line items. Your utility must itemise on request. A rise in the supply or capacity line is a market event; a rise in riders is a policy decision; a rise in the fixed charge is a rate case.
  2. Compare to last August, not last month. Seasonal load swamps everything. The relevant comparison is the same month a year earlier — which is how the table above is built.
  3. Check whether your state has a docket open. The tracker lists 23 states, thresholds and case numbers. Intervention is free and public comment takes ten minutes.
  4. Ask for the budget billing recalculation. If your utility levelised you, a spike is spread rather than billed — but the spread is based on an estimate that may now be wrong in your favour.
  5. Check the fixed charge, not just the rate. A rate held flat while the customer charge doubles is a rate increase wearing a flat-rate costume. Several states have approved exactly that this year.

The bottom line

The table is the argument. A country that can charge 12.72 cents in one state and 47.18 in another is not running an energy market, it is running fifty-one of them, and the ones that let load arrive without a rule are the ones now sending households the bill.

Six states wrote the rule first. They are not cheaper. They are slower to get dearer, which over a decade is the same thing.

Questions

Which state has the cheapest electricity?

Nevada, at 12.72 cents per kilowatt-hour in August 2026 — and it is also the only state in the country whose rate fell over the year. North Dakota, Utah, Idaho and Louisiana follow, all under 13.5 cents. All five are hydro, nuclear or low-cost balancing authorities with small data center footprints.

Which state has the most expensive?

Hawaii, at 47.18 cents — 2.6 times the national average and 3.7 times Nevada. Massachusetts, Rhode Island and Connecticut follow, all above 24 cents, all with constrained import capacity.

Why did my electric bill go up so much?

If you are in Maryland, Maine, Rhode Island, Pennsylvania or Ohio, most of it is PJM capacity charge passed through by your utility — roughly $10 to $11 a month attributable in Ohio and western Maryland. If you are in California or New England, it is generation cost and import constraint. The two look identical on a bill and have nothing to do with each other.

Do data centers raise household bills?

Nationally, the average effect is small and possibly negative. Regionally, inside PJM, it is documented and already being billed. Both are true, which is why the national argument never resolves.

Does a data center tariff lower my rate?

No. It slows the rise. Six states with published thresholds averaged +7.0% over the year against +7.6% for five PJM states without one. The gap is inside the noise and one year cannot separate tariff design from fuel and capacity effects.

Why is Hawaii so expensive?

Islanded grids. Hawaii cannot import power from anywhere, and most generation burns oil delivered by ship. Every kilowatt-hour carries the fuel and the freight — which is also why Hawaii's 12.3% rise is a fuel story, not a data center story.

What is the US average?

18.16 cents per kilowatt-hour in August 2026, up 3.8% from 17.5 cents a year earlier, per EIA Table 5.6.A.

Is this data final?

No. EIA treats 2026 as preliminary and revises. The table is regenerated on each monthly release and the revision date is at the foot of the page.
