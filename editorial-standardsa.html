Editorial standards

These are the rules. They apply to every piece of work we publish, in every format, by staff and freelancers alike — and they're written to be enforced, not admired.

Principles

Four rules sit above all the others. Everything below is detail attached to them.

  1. Accuracy before speed. We'd rather be late than first and wrong. Nothing publishes that an editor hasn't read.
  2. Show the working. Claims carry sources; numbers carry links; estimates carry ranges. A reader should be able to retrace a story to its documents.
  3. Name the accountability. A human being is responsible for every published sentence, and their name is on it.
  4. Correct in public. Fixes are visible, dated and permanent. Silent edits are the most common way newsrooms lose trust.

Sourcing and verification

A source is evidence, not a person who agrees with us. Ranked in order of preference:

  1. Primary documents — statutes, court filings, agency rulemaking, budget tables, BLS and Census series, WHO and UN datasets, peer-reviewed papers, company filings.
  2. On-the-record named sources with direct knowledge.
  3. Expert sources speaking to their field, with the limits of their expertise stated.
  4. Other outlets — a tip to chase, never the end of the chain. If a story exists only elsewhere, we say who broke it and link to them.
Two-source rule

Any contested factual claim needs two independent sources, or one primary document. "Independent" means they didn't get it from each other.

Where a claim can't be verified, we publish it as a claim — attributed, qualified, and flagged as unverified in the copy rather than smoothed over.

Anonymous sources

Anonymity is granted by an editor, not claimed by a reporter. When we use it, the copy explains three things: why the person can't be named, what they'd risk by being named, and where their interest lies.

  • Prefer "a person familiar with the negotiations" to "sources say".
  • Never use anonymity to launder an opinion into a fact.
  • The reporter must know the source's real identity and be able to vouch for it to the editor.
  • If an anonymous source turns out to be central to a story, we name them once doing so is safe.

Numbers and data

  • Every statistic links to the document or dataset it came from, with the reference period stated.
  • Rates beat raw counts; per-capita beats totals; ranges beat point estimates when uncertainty exists.
  • Charts state source, sample size and date range. Seasonal adjustment is disclosed. Truncated axes are labelled.
  • We check the arithmetic ourselves. A number we can't reproduce doesn't run.
  • Where the official figure is out of date, we say how out of date it is rather than freshening it silently.

AI policy

We use generative models as tools, not authors. The dividing line is simple: a model may propose, a human must dispose.

Permitted

  • Drafting structure, outlines and scaffolding for explainers.
  • Summarising long documents a reporter has already read, to locate passages.
  • Transcription, headline variants, SEO metadata, social copy variants.
  • First-pass data tidying, with the result checked against the source table.

Required checks

  • Every figure a model produces is verified against the primary source before publication.
  • Every quotation is checked against the transcript or recording — models reword quotes and invent punctuation.
  • Every citation is opened. Models fabricate plausible-looking case names and papers.
  • An editor reads the final text in full and is accountable for it.

Disclosure

  • Where a piece was substantially drafted with a model, the byline block says so.
  • AI-generated images are captioned as AI-generated, never passed off as photographs.
  • Data visualisations produced with model assistance are still checked by a human and credited to them.
Never
  • Publish an AI-drafted quote that hasn't been checked against the source.
  • Use a model to "fill" a missing fact without a source behind it.
  • Let a model invent a source, a title or a date.
  • Use AI to fabricate or alter a photograph of a real event.

Health and sex-education review

Health, reproductive health and sex-education content is reviewed before publication by a clinician or licensed practitioner, named on the page. Review covers factual accuracy, the currency of guidance and age-appropriateness of framing.

  • Guidance is tied to the issuing body and its date — [e.g. CDC, WHO, ACOG, AAP].
  • Where bodies disagree, the disagreement is reported rather than averaged.
  • Prevalence figures name their sample and year.
  • Reviewer credentials are stated in the byline, with any conflict of interest disclosed.
  • Reviewed pages carry a review date and are re-checked at least annually, sooner if guidance changes.

Labels: news, analysis, opinion

Readers are entitled to know what kind of thing they're reading.

  • News — verified fact, attributed. The reporter's view stays out of it.
  • Analysis — interpretation built on verified fact, with the evidence visible.
  • Opinion — an argued position, signed, and factually checked all the same.

Every piece carries one of these labels at the top. Sponsored content is labelled separately and never dressed as editorial.

Independence and conflicts

  • Staff disclose financial interests, directorships, paid speaking and political donations to the Standards Desk annually.
  • No gifts beyond trivial value; travel funded by an interested party is disclosed in the copy.
  • Advertisers get no sight of editorial copy and no right of approval. Sponsored pieces are clearly marked and produced under the same standards.
  • A reporter's personal stake in a story is disclosed in the piece.
  • Freelancers may not file to us and a direct competitor on the same commissioned piece.

Corrections

Corrections policy in full lives at Corrections. The essentials:

  • Factual errors are fixed on the page with a dated note saying what changed.
  • Substantive changes to a live story are logged at its foot. Cosmetic fixes move the "updated" stamp only.
  • We correct whether or not anyone complained, and we thank the person who flagged it.
  • A correction that undermines a story's premise triggers a review of the whole piece, not just the sentence.

Email corrections@pulseamericanews.com with the URL and the claim. Acknowledged within one working day.

Images and graphics

  • Original photography is credited to the photographer. Licensed stock is credited in the caption with the licence source.
  • Library or file pictures of a person or place are captioned with the date they were taken.
  • Cropping that changes meaning is avoided; if unavoidable, the caption says what was outside the frame.
  • Handout photos are labelled "handout".
  • AI-generated images are labelled and never used where a real photograph of the specific event exists.

Social, video and headlines

The same standards travel with the story onto every platform. A social post that overstates the article is a factual error.

  • Headlines must be supported by the body copy — no tension manufactured for clicks.
  • Corrections are pushed to the platform where the wrong version ran.
  • Numbers in social cards are re-checked separately; card copy is a publication surface, not decoration.
  • Aggregation posts credit and link the originator.

Freelancers and contributors

  • Freelancers are briefed on this page before their first commission and are bound by all of it.
  • They must disclose any interest in the subject and any prior version of the piece elsewhere.
  • Fact-checking responsibility sits with the writer and the commissioning editor jointly.
  • Plagiarism is grounds for withheld payment and removal from the contributor list.

Diversity and sourcing breadth

Homogeneous sourcing produces confidently wrong coverage. We track who we talk to, not as a box-ticking exercise but because it changes what we find.

  • We seek sources affected by a story, not only officials who describe it.
  • On place-based stories, at least one local voice is sourced wherever reachable, and the effort is recorded.
  • Expert sourcing is deliberately drawn beyond a small recurring roster of institutions.
  • Where a beat's sourcing skews one way, the editor says so at review.
  • Access barriers — time zones, translation, connectivity — are budgeted for rather than used as excuses.

Complaints and escalation

  1. Stage one — email corrections@pulseamericanews.com. The handling editor responds within one working day.
  2. Stage two — unresolved after five working days, escalate to the Standards Desk at standards@pulseamericanews.com.
  3. Stage three — final review by the Editor-in-Chief. Outcomes are recorded on the corrections log.

Legal complaints run in parallel and don't pause stages one to three. Where a complaint shows a systemic failure, this page is amended and the amendment dated.

Review of this page

Standards rot when nobody reads them. This page is reviewed quarterly by the Editor-in-Chief with the Standards Desk, and after any correction that reveals a gap. Amendments are dated and listed below.
